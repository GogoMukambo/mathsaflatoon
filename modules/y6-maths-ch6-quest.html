Collecting Data

Master the art of sorting data, investigating trends, and sampling populations.

Why learn this in the real world?

Data is everywhere. Companies use it to track sales and improve customer satisfaction, hospitals use it to improve patient care, and scientists use it to test predictions. Knowing how to correctly collect, categorize, and sample data allows you to separate fact from fiction and make informed, evidence-based decisions in the real world.

1 Data Types

Discrete Data

  • Discrete data can take particular values only.
  • It is usually data that is counted in whole numbers.
  • Example: The number of brothers you have (0, 1, 2, 3...). You cannot have 1.5 brothers.
  • Example: Your shoe size, or the number of pages in a book.

Continuous Data

  • Continuous data can take any numerical value within a range.
  • It is data that is measured.
  • Example: Mass, length, and time. E.g., The mass of a baby could be 3.45 kg.

Categorical Data

  • Categorical data uses words or labels, not numerical values.
  • Example: The sports you like to watch, or your favorite subject.
  • Example: Eye color, or the type of vegetables in a recipe.

2 Investigations

Statistical Questions & Predictions

  • A statistical question expects a variety of answers. (e.g., "What is the average mass of a baby?")
  • Before investigating, you often make a prediction to test. (e.g., "11-year-old girls run faster than boys.")

Methods of Collecting Data

Depending on your prediction, you choose the best way to collect data:

  • Measurements/Experiments: For testing plant growth in the sun vs shade.
  • Questionnaires/Surveys: Good for gathering opinions or tracking habits. Always ensure questions are clear, cover all possibilities (no gaps in multiple choice), and avoid overlapping options.
  • Observations: Watching traffic flow or recording if teachers wear glasses.
  • Interviews: Asking people directly about their experiences.

Critiquing a Questionnaire

Flawed Question: "How many hours of homework do you do? [1-2 hours] [2-3 hours] [More than 3]"

  • Flaw 1: Overlapping boxes (if I do exactly 2 hours, which box do I tick?).
  • Flaw 2: Missing options (what if I do less than 1 hour or no homework at all?).

3 Taking a Sample

Population vs. Sample

  • The population is the entire group of people or objects you want to investigate (e.g. ALL newborn babies).
  • A sample is a smaller group chosen to represent the population. It is used when testing the whole population would take too long or cost too much.

Sample Size & Representation

  • The sample should not be too small (e.g. 5 people), or it will not accurately represent the whole population.
  • A large sample size is more reliable, but you must consider the extra time, effort, and cost involved in collecting the data.

Solved Examples

Arun investigates how long learners in his school hold their breath.

  • Population: All the learners in the school.
  • Why sample?: Testing every single learner would take a very long time and interrupt classes.
  • How to sample?: Put names in a hat and select 40 learners randomly.
