Lines and Angles

Master points, line segments, lines, rays, angle classification, degree measures, and protractors.

1 Points, Line Segments, Lines & Rays

Building Blocks of Plane Geometry (Sections 2.1 – 2.4)

  • Point: Determines an exact location (like the tip of a compass or needle) with no length, breadth, or height. Denoted by a capital letter: Point P, Point Z, Point T.
  • Line Segment (AB or BA): The shortest path between two points A and B (called its end points).
  • Line (AB or line m): A line segment extended endlessly in both directions. Infinitely many lines pass through 1 point, but exactly 1 unique line passes through 2 distinct points!
  • Ray (AP): Starts at a fixed starting point A and goes endlessly in one direction through P. Ray OA cannot be written as Ray AO because the starting point must come first!

1 Angles, Vertex, Arms & Superimposition

Understanding & Comparing Angles (Sections 2.5 – 2.7)

  • Angle (∠DBE or ∠EBD): Formed by two rays (the arms BD and BE) sharing a common starting point (the vertex B). The vertex letter is always written in the middle!
  • Why can’t ∠APB be called ∠P when 3 rays meet at P? Because multiple angles (∠APB, ∠BPC, ∠APC) share vertex P, so ∠P is ambiguous!
  • Size of an Angle = Amount of Rotation: Shortening or lengthening the arms (like snipping paper straws) never changes the angle size!
  • Comparing Angles: We can compare angles by superimposition (overlapping vertices and one arm) or by placing a transparent circle centred at the vertex.

Solved Example: Lines & Angles from 3 and 4 Points (Page 20–21)

3 Non-Collinear Points (A, B, C):

• Pairs of points give 3 lines: AB, BC, and CA.
• They form 3 interior angles: ∠ABC, ∠BCA, ∠CAB.

4 Points (No 3 on a Line: A, B, C, D):

• Pairs of points give 6 lines: AB, BC, CD, DA, AC, BD.
• Using letters A, B, C, D, we can name 12 angles (3 at each vertex)!

2 Special Angles & Classifying Angles

Five Key Types of Angles (Sections 2.8 & 2.11)

  • Full Turn (Complete Angle = 360°): One complete rotation around a circle.
  • Straight Angle (180°): Half of a full turn (½ turn); the two arms form a straight line.
  • Right Angle (90°): Half of a straight angle (¼ of a full turn); forms an ‘L’ corner. Two lines meeting at 90° are perpendicular lines.
  • Acute Angle (0° < angle < 90°): Sharp angle smaller than a right angle (less than a quarter turn).
  • Obtuse Angle (90° < angle < 180°): Blunt angle greater than 90° and less than 180°.
  • Reflex Angle (180° < angle < 360°): Greater than a straight angle and less than a full turn!

2 Degree Measures (360°) & Paper Folding Bisectors

  • Why 360 Degrees? Ancient texts like the Rigveda (Verse 1.164.48) describe a wheel with 360 spokes. Practically, 360 is the smallest number evenly divisible by every number from 1 to 10 except 7 (plus 12 and 24)!
  • Dividing a 360° Circle into Equal Parts (Page 34):
    1 part = 360°, 2 parts = 180°, 3 parts = 120°, 4 parts = 90°, 5 parts = 72°, 6 parts = 60°, 8 parts = 45°, 9 parts = 40°, 10 parts = 36°, 12 parts = 30°.
  • Angle Bisector & Handmade Protractor (Pages 38–40): Folding an angle in half is called bisecting the angle:
    Full turn (360°) → ½ turn (180°) → ¼ turn (90°) → ⅛ turn (45°) → 1/16 turn (22.5°)!

Solved Example: Handmade Semicircle Protractor (Fig. 2.20)

When a 180° semicircle is folded in half 3 times, it is divided into 8 equal sectors of 180° ÷ 8 = 22.5° each! The creases mark: 0°, 22.5°, 45°, 67.5°, 90°, 112.5°, 135°, 157.5°, and 180°.

3 Reading Protractors & Subtracting Scales

  • Two Scales (Inner & Outer): A standard protractor has two sets of markings from 0° to 180° (one increasing right-to-left, and one left-to-right) so you can measure angles opening in either direction!
  • Measuring by Subtraction: If two rays pass through 20° and 55° on the same scale, the angle between them is 55° − 20° = 35°.
  • Measuring Reflex Angles (Page 41 Q4): Since a standard protractor goes up to 180°, measure the smaller unmarked interior angle (e.g., 100°) and subtract from 360°: 360° − 100° = 260°!

Solved Example: Multi-Ray Protractor (Page 42 Q6)

• ∠AXB (outer scale from A) = 65°
• ∠CXE (inner scale from E) = 85°
• ∠BXE (inner scale from E) = 115°
• ∠BXC = 115° − 85° = 30°

3 Clocks, Ashoka Chakra & Angle Puzzles

  • Angles in a Clock (Page 45): A 360° clock face has 12 equal hour divisions, so each hour step is 360° ÷ 12 = 30°!
    → 1 o’clock = 30°, 2 o’clock = 60°, 3 o’clock = 90°, 4 o’clock = 120°, 6 o’clock = 180°.
  • The Ashoka Chakra (Page 54 Q6): Has 24 equally spaced spokes.
    → Angle between two adjacent spokes = 360° ÷ 24 = 15°.
    → Largest acute angle between two spokes = 5 × 15° = 75° (since 6 × 15° = 90° is a right angle!).
  • Triangle Angle Sum (Page 43 Q9): Adding the three interior angles of any triangle always gives 180°!

Solved Example: The Textbook Angle Puzzle (Page 54 Q7)

“I am an acute angle. If you double, triple, or quadruple (4×) my measure, you still get an acute angle (< 90°)! But if you multiply my measure by 5, you get an obtuse angle (> 90°).”

  • Since 4 × angle < 90°, the whole-number angle must be 22° or less (4 × 22° = 88°).
  • Since 5 × angle > 90°, the angle must be greater than 18° (5 × 18° = 90°).
  • Possible integer measures: 19°, 20°, 21°, and 22°!
