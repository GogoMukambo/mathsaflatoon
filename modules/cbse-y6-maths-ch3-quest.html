1 Taller Neighbours & Supercells (3.1 – 3.2)

How Numbers Describe Neighbours

  • Taller Neighbours Rule (Section 3.1): Children stand in a line and say how many of their immediate neighbours are taller than them:
    • ‘0’ if neither neighbour is taller, ‘1’ if only one neighbour is taller, and ‘2’ if both neighbours are taller.
    • Children standing at the two ends can never say ‘2’ (they have only 1 neighbour!). For 5 children of different heights, at most 2 children can say ‘2’ (arranged Tall–Short–Tall–Short–Tall: 0, 2, 0, 2, 0).
  • Supercells (Section 3.2): A cell in a 1D row or 2D grid is a supercell if its number is strictly greater than all its adjacent cells (left/right, and top/bottom in 2D).
  • Key Supercell Laws: The largest number in a table is always a supercell; the smallest number can never be a supercell! In a 1D row of n cells, the maximum number of supercells is n/2 (if n is even) or (n + 1)/2 (if n is odd, so 9 cells can have at most 5 supercells).

2 Playing with Digits & Palindromes (3.4 – 3.5)

Digit Counts, Digit Sums & Palindromic Numbers

  • How Many k-Digit Numbers Exist? (Page 60):
    1-digit: 9 (1–9) • 2-digit: 90 (10–99) • 3-digit: 900 (100–999) • 4-digit: 9,000 • 5-digit: 90,000.
  • Digit Sum 14 (Page 60): Smallest number with digit sum 14 is 59 (5+9=14). Largest 5-digit number with digit sum 14 is 95,000. Consecutive 3-digit numbers (123→6, 234→9, 345→12) always have digit sums that are multiples of 3!
  • Digit Detective ‘7’ (Page 61): Writing numbers from 1 to 100 uses the digit ‘7’ 20 times; from 1 to 1000 uses the digit ‘7’ 300 times!
  • Palindromes (Pages 61–62): Read the same forwards and backwards (e.g., 66, 848, 1221). Using only digits {1, 2, 3}, there are 9 possible 3-digit palindromes (111, 121, 131, 212, 222, 232, 313, 323, 333).

“I am a 5-digit palindrome (tth, th, h, t, u). I am an odd number. My ‘t’ digit is double my ‘u’ digit. My ‘h’ digit is double my ‘t’ digit. Who am I?”

3. By palindrome symmetry: 12,421 (Twelve thousand four hundred twenty-one)!

2 Kaprekar’s Constant (6174) & Clock Numbers (3.6 – 3.7)

  • The Magic Number of D.R. Kaprekar (Page 63): Pick any 4-digit number with at least two different digits. Arrange digits to form the largest number (A) and smallest number (B), then subtract C = A − B. Repeat with the digits of C:
    → You ALWAYS reach the Kaprekar constant 6174 (7641 − 1467 = 6174)!
  • 3-Digit Kaprekar Constant (Page 63 Explore): Carrying out the exact same steps on a 3-digit number (like 321) always reaches and repeats 495 (954 − 459 = 495)!
  • Clock & Calendar Patterns (Pages 64–65):
    • From 10:01, the next palindromic time is 11:11 (after 70 minutes), and the one after that is 12:21 (after 140 minutes from 10:01).
    • Smallest 5-digit palindrome (10,001) + Largest 5-digit palindrome (99,999) = 110,000; their difference is 89,998.

3 Mental Math, Digit Bounds & Pattern Sums (3.8 – 3.9)

  • Mental Math Target Sums (Pages 65–66): Using 25,000; 400; 13,000; 1,500; 60,000, we can form 38,800 = 25,000 + 13,000 + 400×2 and 19,500 = 13,000 + 1,500×4 + 400... (or 1,500×13). However, 1,000 cannot be made because the only number under 1,000 is 400, and 1,000 is not a multiple of 400!
  • Impossible Digit Operations (“Never True”, Pages 66–67):
    • 4-digit + 4-digit = 6-digit sum: Never possible! (Max is 9,999 + 9,999 = 19,998, a 5-digit number).
    • 5-digit + 5-digit = 18,500: Never possible! (Min is 10,000 + 10,000 = 20,000).
    • 5-digit − 5-digit = 91,500: Never possible! (Max difference is 99,999 − 10,000 = 89,999).
    • 5-digit − 2-digit = 3-digit: Never possible! (Min is 10,000 − 99 = 9,901, a 4-digit number).
  • Quick Pattern Sums (Section 3.9, Pages 67–68): Multiply count × value instead of adding one by one!
    • Fig (a): (12 × 40) + (10 × 50) = 480 + 500 = 980.   • Fig (c): (32 × 32) + (16 × 64) = 1024 + 1024 = 2048.

3 Collatz Conjecture, Estimation & Game of 21 (3.10 – 3.12)

  • The Collatz Conjecture (Section 3.10, Pages 68–69): Start with any whole number:
    • If it is even, take half of it (n ÷ 2).
    • If it is odd, multiply by 3 and add 1 (3n + 1).
    → Example starting at 12: 12 → 6 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1!
  • Simple Estimation (Section 3.11, Pages 69–71): Sheetal (in Grade 6) claims she has spent 13,000 hours in school. At 6 hours/day × 200 school days/year = 1,200 hours/year, 8 years (Nursery to Grade 6) is about 9,600 hours (13,000 hours would take 10.8 years, so her estimate is too high!).
  • Winning Strategies in Number Games (Section 3.12, Pages 71–73):
    • Game #1 (Reach 21, adding 1, 2, or 3): Player 1 always wins by saying 1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 21 (step of 1+3 = 4).
    • Game #2 (Reach 99, adding 1 to 10): Player 2 always wins by saying multiples of 11: 11, 22, 33, …, 99!

Solved Example: All-Odd-Digits 5-Digit Mystery (Page 72 Q3)

Among 5-digit numbers between 35,000 and 75,000 such that all digits are odd (1, 3, 5, 7, 9):
• Largest number: 73,999 (or 73,951 without repeating digits).
• Smallest number: 35,111 (or 35,179 without repeating digits).
• Closest to 50,000: 51,111 (difference of 1,111, which is closer than 39,999!).
