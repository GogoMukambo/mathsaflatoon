**Learner’s Book 8**

## 1 Integers

*Number*

Let's build with numbers! We'll learn how to break numbers down into their 'prime' building blocks, how to handle negative numbers without getting confused, and how to use powers and roots like a pro.

### 1.1 Factors, multiples and primes

**Key idea: What is so special about primes?**

Primes are the building blocks of whole numbers!
KEYWORD: prime — only divides by 1 and itself. Like 2, 3, 5, 7, 47.
KEYWORD: integer — a whole number. No fractions, no decimals.
Every whole number bigger than 1 is either prime, or built by multiplying primes.
Example: 46 = 2 × 23
Example: 47 is prime
Example: 48 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3
Example: 49 = 7 × 7
Example: 50 = 2 × 5 × 5
KEYWORD: 1 is not prime — too lonely to count!

**Recall: Crack 120 into primes.**

Grow a factor tree! Split, circle the primes, stop when the ends are all primes.
KEYWORD: factor tree — a splitting diagram that ends on primes.
KEYWORD: prime factor — a prime that multiplies to make the number.
KEYWORD: index — the little number that counts repeats. 2³ means three 2s.
Example: 120 = 12 × 10
Example: 12 = 3 × 4 and 10 = 2 × 5
Example: 4 = 2 × 2
FORMULA: 120 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 = 2³ × 3 × 5
Example: 75 = 3 × 5²
Never put 1 on a branch.

**Check: HCF and LCM of 120 and 75 — what’s the trick?**

Line up the prime ingredients.
KEYWORD: HCF — highest common factor. The biggest chunk both numbers share.
KEYWORD: LCM — lowest common multiple. The first shared multiple.
FORMULA: 120 = 2³ × 3 × 5
FORMULA: 75 = 3 × 5²
FORMULA: LCM = bigger pile of each prime = 2³ × 3 × 5² = 600
FORMULA: HCF = smaller pile of each prime = 3 × 5 = 15

### 1.2 Multiplying and dividing integers

**Key idea: What happens when two negatives multiply?**

Watch the pattern — it spills the secret! Keep −3. Knock the other number down by 1. The answer jumps up by 3 each time.
KEYWORD: integer — a whole number. +, − or 0.
Example: −3 × 4 = −12
Example: −3 × 3 = −9
Example: −3 × 2 = −6
Example: −3 × −1 = 3
Example: −3 × −2 = 6
Two minuses hug and turn into a plus!
FORMULA: same signs → +
FORMULA: different signs → −
Example: 2 × −9 = −18
Example: −6 × −4 = 24

**Recall: Work out (−3 + 15) ÷ −4.**

Brackets first — they are the boss! Then × or ÷, and use the sign rule.
KEYWORD: BIDMAS — Brackets, Indices, ÷ and ×, + and −.
Example: (3 + 5) × −4 = 8 × −4 = −32
Example: (−3 + 15) ÷ −4 = 12 ÷ −4 = −3
Example: −4 × (5 − 8) = −4 × −3 = 12
Example: (3 × −4) ÷ −2 = −12 ÷ −2 = 6

**Check: How do you undo a multiplication?**

÷ is just × played backwards. Flip the product into two divisions. Same sign rule still works!
KEYWORD: inverse — the opposite move. ÷ undoes ×.
FORMULA: −4 × 6 = −24 → −24 ÷ 6 = −4
FORMULA: −4 × 6 = −24 → −24 ÷ −4 = 6
Example: −7 × −2 = 14 → 14 ÷ −2 = −7
Example: −7 × −2 = 14 → 14 ÷ −7 = −2
Example: 18 ÷ −6 = −3
Example: −28 ÷ −4 = 7

### 1.3 Square roots and cube roots

**Key idea: How many square roots does a number have?**

Two — a plus twin and a minus twin!

KEYWORD: square root — who times themselves makes this?

Example: 5 × 5 = 25, so √25 = 5.

Counting numbers keep only the plus one.

Example: −5 × −5 = 25 too. Two minuses hug, so −5 is also a root.

FORMULA: positives get two roots, + and −

Grumpy negatives get none. A square never goes negative.

FORMULA: x² = −25 has no solution

**Recall: Solve x² = 64, x³ = 64 and x³ + 64 = 0.**

Squares can be twins. Cubes are one-and-done.

KEYWORD: cube root — times itself three times.

Example: 5 × 5 × 5 = 125, so ∛125 = 5.

Example: −5 × −5 × −5 = −125, so ∛(−125) = −5. Not +5!

Every number — +, − or 0 — has just one cube root.

FORMULA: x² = 64 → x = 8 or x = −8

FORMULA: x³ = 64 → x = 4

FORMULA: x³ + 64 = 0 → x³ = −64 → x = −4

**Check: Sort 5, −1/5, −500, 16, −4.8, 99½ into N, I and Q.**

Nesting boxes!

KEYWORD: natural numbers N — counting numbers and 0.

KEYWORD: integer I — whole numbers. +, − or 0. No bits.

KEYWORD: rational number Q — can be written as a fraction.

Example: N in the list → 5, 16

Example: I in the list → 5, −500, 16

Example: Q → the whole list, even −1/5, −4.8 and 99½

FORMULA: N lives inside I. I lives inside Q.

### 1.4 Indices

**Key idea: Why is 5⁰ just 1?**

A power is a shortcut for “times itself again!”
KEYWORD: index — the little number on top. In 5³ it means three 5s.
Example: 5³ = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125
Example: 5⁴ = 125 × 5 = 625
Slide left and divide by 5.
Example: 25 ÷ 5 = 5, so 5¹ = 5
Example: 5 ÷ 5 = 1, so 5⁰ = 1. Boom!
FORMULA: any positive whole number to the power 0 is 1
Example: 7³ = 343
Example: 7⁴ = 2401
Example: 7⁰ = 1. Easy!

**Recall: Write 4 × 16 as powers of 2. What’s the trick?**

Same team (same base)? The little numbers high-five and ADD!
KEYWORD: generalise — spot the pattern, then write a rule that always works.
Example: 4 × 16 = 64 is 2² × 2⁴ = 2⁶
Example: 8 × 4 = 32 is 2³ × 2² = 2⁵
Example: 6² × 6³ = 6⁵
FORMULA: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
Different bases? Don’t add.
Example: 2³ × 5³ is not 2⁸

**Check: Write 32 ÷ 4 as powers of 2. What is 5⁶ ÷ 5⁶?**

Division is the opposite slide. Same team? SUBTRACT the little numbers.
Example: 32 ÷ 4 = 8 is 2⁵ ÷ 2² = 2³
Example: 2⁷ ÷ 2⁵ = 2²
FORMULA: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
Same power top and bottom? A number ÷ itself is just 1.
Example: 5⁶ ÷ 5⁶ = 5⁰ = 1
Brackets make the little numbers multiply.
Example: (3²)³ = 3⁶
FORMULA: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ

## 2 Expressions, formulae and equations

*Algebra*

Algebra is like a secret code! We'll learn how to swap numbers for letters, multiply brackets, and solve mystery equations like a detective.

### 2.1 Constructing expressions

**Key idea: What are the parts of an expression?**

A 'variable' is a letter (like x). A 'coefficient' is the number stuck to the letter (like the 3 in 3x). A 'constant' is a normal number on its own (like +8).

**Recall: Think of a number y, divide by 3, add 5, then multiply the whole result by 2.**

2(y/3 + 5). (The brackets mean 'do this first!').

**Check: Why is 4x − 7 called 'linear', but x² is not?**

Because 'linear' means the letters don't have any powers like squared (²) or cubed (³).

### 2.2 Using expressions and formulae

**Key idea: What does 'substituting' mean?**

It just means swapping the letters for real numbers! Then do the math like normal.

**Recall: v = u + 10t. Find v when u = 8 and t = 15.**

Swap the letters: v = 8 + 10 × 15. Do the multiplying first! 8 + 150 = 158.

**Check: Make t the boss (the subject) of v = u + 10t.**

Move the u over: v - u = 10t. Then divide by 10: t = (v − u) / 10.

### 2.3 Expanding brackets

**Key idea: How do you expand a bracket?**

Multiply the thing on the OUTSIDE by EVERY thing on the INSIDE! a(b + c) becomes ab + ac.

**Recall: Expand 4(2a − 5).**

4 × 2a = 8a. 4 × -5 = -20. So it's 8a − 20.

**Check: Expand and simplify 4(2x + 3x²) − x(6 + x).**

8x + 12x² − 6x − x². Then group the like terms: 11x² + 2x.

### 2.4 Factorising

**Key idea: What is factorising?**

It's the exact opposite of expanding! You find the biggest thing they both share (the HCF) and pull it OUTSIDE the brackets.

**Recall: Factorise 6x² + 15x.**

They both divide by 3, and they both have an 'x'. So pull out 3x: 3x(2x + 5).

**Check: How do you check if your factorising is right?**

Just expand the brackets again! You should get exactly what you started with.

### 2.5 Constructing and solving equations

**Key idea: What is an equation?**

It's a scale that is perfectly balanced! To find the mystery letter, do the exact same reverse math to BOTH sides until the letter is alone.

**Recall: Opposite sides of a rectangle are 3(x + 3) cm and 24 cm. Find x.**

They must be equal! 3(x + 3) = 24. Expand: 3x + 9 = 24. Subtract 9: 3x = 15. Divide by 3: x = 5.

**Check: Solve 5y − 4 = 3y + 8.**

Move the small y's over: 2y - 4 = 8. Add 4: 2y = 12. Divide by 2: y = 6.

### 2.6 Inequalities

**Key idea: What do the inequality alligator mouths mean?**

< means strictly less than. ≤ means less than OR equal to. > means strictly greater than. ≥ means greater than OR equal to. The mouth always eats the bigger number!

**Recall: How do you draw −2 ≤ x < 3 on a number line?**

A solid colored dot at −2 (because it's included), and an empty circle dot at 3 (because it's not). Draw a line between them!

**Check: Which whole numbers (integers) fit in −2 ≤ x < 3?**

−2, −1, 0, 1 and 2. (Not 3!).

## 3 Place value and rounding

*Number*

Imagine numbers sliding left and right on a giant grid! We'll learn how to multiply and divide by tiny decimals like 0.1, and how to round numbers so they are easier to use.

### 3.1 Multiplying and dividing by 0.1 and 0.01

**Key idea: What happens when you multiply by 0.1 or 0.01?**

Multiplying by 0.1 is exactly the same as dividing by 10—the number gets ten times smaller! Multiplying by 0.01 is like dividing by 100. The digits slide to the right on the place value grid.

**Key idea: What happens when you divide by a tiny decimal?**

Dividing by 0.1 is the same as multiplying by 10—the number gets ten times bigger! Dividing by 0.01 is like multiplying by 100. The digits slide to the left.

**Recall: Work out 32 × 0.1.**

3.2. The digits slide one place to the right, so 32 becomes 3.2.

**Recall: Work out 4.2 × 0.01.**

0.042. The digits slide two places to the right. We add zeros to fill the empty spaces.

**Check: Work out 6 ÷ 0.1 and 4.156 ÷ 0.01.**

60 (because 6 × 10 = 60) and 415.6 (because 4.156 × 100 = 415.6).

### 3.2 Rounding

**Key idea: What is a 'significant figure'?**

It's the most important digit in a number! The first significant figure is always the first non-zero digit you see when reading from left to right.

**Key idea: How do we round to a significant figure?**

Find the significant figure, then look at the bossy digit right next to it. If the bossy digit is 5 or more, round up! If it's 4 or less, keep it the same. Remember to put zeros in the empty spaces so the number stays about the same size.

**Recall: Round 4286 to 1 significant figure.**

4000. The first significant figure is 4. The bossy digit next to it is 2. Since 2 is small, the 4 stays the same, and the rest become zeros.

**Check: Round 0.08069 to 3 significant figures.**

0.0807. Ignore the starting zeros. The first significant figure is 8, the second is 0, the third is 6. The bossy digit next to 6 is 9, so the 6 rounds up to 7.

**Check: Round 0.7963 to 2 significant figures.**

0.80. The 9 rounds up to 10, which bumps the 7 up to 8. We keep the zero at the end (0.80) to show we rounded to 2 figures!

## 4 Decimals

*Number*

Let's learn how to line up decimals to see which is biggest, and how to multiply or divide them without getting confused by the dot!

### 4.1 Ordering decimals

**Key idea: How do we find out which decimal is bigger?**

Line up the decimal dots like buttons on a shirt! Add zeros to the end so they are the same length, then compare them from left to right. Also, make sure measurements are in the same unit first!

**Recall: Put these in order, smallest first: −4.76, −4.05, −4.67, −4.50.**

−4.76, −4.67, −4.50, −4.05. A negative number with the larger size is further left, so it is smaller.

**Check: Which is larger, 0.307 or 0.32?**

0.32.

### 4.2 Multiplying decimals

**Key idea: How do we multiply decimals?**

Pretend the decimal dots aren't there and multiply like normal whole numbers! Then, count how many digits were behind the dots in the question, and put the dot back so the answer has the same number of digits behind it.

**Recall: Work out 5.96 × 0.35.**

596 × 35 = 20860. There are 4 decimal places in total, so the product is 2.0860, which is 2.086.

**Check: How can you check 5.96 × 0.35?**

Estimate 6 × 0.4 = 2.4, which is close to 2.086.

### 4.3 Dividing by decimals

**Key idea: How do we divide by a decimal?**

We don't like dividing by decimals, so we use a trick! Multiply both numbers by 10, 100, or 1000 until the number you are dividing by becomes a normal whole number.

**Recall: Work out 3.468 ÷ 0.8.**

34.68 ÷ 8 = 4.335.

**Check: Work out 8.4 ÷ 0.2.**

84 ÷ 2 = 42.

### 4.4 Making decimal calculations easier

**Key idea: How can we make tricky decimal math easier?**

We can split the decimal into easier pieces! For example, 0.9 is just 1 minus 0.1. So 22 × 0.9 is the same as 22 × 1 minus 22 × 0.1.

**Recall: Work out 4 × 12.6 × 2.5 by regrouping.**

(4 × 2.5) × 12.6 = 10 × 12.6 = 126.

**Check: Work out 12 × 0.15 by splitting 0.15.**

12 × 0.5 × 0.3 = 6 × 0.3 = 1.8.

## 5 Angles and constructions

*Geometry and measure*

Let's become geometry detectives! We'll find hidden angles using secret letter shapes like F, Z, and C, and learn how to draw perfect shapes using just a compass and ruler.

### 5.1 Parallel lines

**Key idea: How do we find hidden angles on parallel lines?**

Look for secret letters! Corresponding angles make an 'F' shape and are equal. Alternate angles make a 'Z' shape and are equal. Co-interior angles make a 'C' shape and add up to 180°!

**Recall: One co-interior angle is 115°. What is the other?**

65°, because they add up to 180° (180 - 115 = 65).

**Check: If angle a is 70°, which other labelled angles equal 70°?**

d, because vertically opposite angles are equal; e, because it corresponds to a; and h, because it corresponds to d.

### 5.2 The exterior angle of a triangle

**Key idea: What is the exterior angle trick?**

If you stretch one side of a triangle out, it makes an 'exterior' angle on the outside. This outside angle is always exactly the same as the two inside angles furthest away from it added together!

**Recall: Why does the outside angle equal the two inside ones?**

All three inside angles add to 180°. The outside angle and its inside neighbor also add to 180° on a straight line. So the outside angle must equal the other two inside ones!

**Check: Interior angles at A and B are 40° and 75°. What is the exterior angle at C?**

115° (because 40 + 75 = 115).

### 5.3 Constructions

**Key idea: How do we draw perfect triangles?**

You need three clues! SSS: three sides (use compass arcs). SAS: two sides and the angle between them. ASA: two angles and the side between them. RHS: a right angle, the longest side, and one other side.

**Recall: How do you cut a line exactly in half at a right angle?**

Open your compass to more than half the line. Draw big arcs from both ends so they cross above and below. Draw a line straight through the crossings!

**Check: How do you cut an angle exactly in half?**

Draw an arc across both arms from the corner. From those two marks, draw two more arcs that cross in the middle. Draw a line from the corner through the cross!

## 6 Collecting data

*Statistics*

Let's become data scientists! We'll learn how to ask the right questions and pick a fair group of people to study so our results are actually useful.

### 6.1 Data collection

**Key idea: What kinds of data are there?**

Categorical data is words (like eye color or favorite pizza). Discrete data is counted whole numbers (like number of pets). Continuous data is measured and can have decimals (like your exact height or time).

**Recall: How can we collect data?**

We can use a questionnaire (survey), interview people, use a tally chart to count things we see, or use a computer data logger.

**Check: Why is 'Do you like our excellent canteen?' a bad question?**

Because the word 'excellent' tries to trick people into saying yes! A fair question should be neutral and not hint at an answer.

### 6.2 Sampling

**Key idea: What is a population and a sample?**

The 'population' is everyone you want to know about (like the whole school). A 'sample' is the smaller group you actually ask. In a 'random sample', it's like picking names from a hat—everyone has a fair chance!

**Recall: What makes a sample 'random'?**

When every single person in the population has the exact same chance of being picked.

**Check: Why is asking only your friends a bad sample?**

Because your friends might all like the same things you do! That makes the sample 'biased'—it doesn't represent what the whole school thinks.

## 7 Fractions

*Number*

Let's slice up some pizzas! We'll learn how to compare fractions, turn them into decimals, and do math with mixed numbers.

### 7.1 Fractions and recurring decimals

**Key idea: When does a fraction's decimal end?**

Only if the bottom number (denominator) is made by multiplying 2s and 5s. If any other prime number sneaks in (like 3, 7, or 11), the decimal will repeat forever!

**Recall: Write 1/3, 5/11 and 1/7 using the repeating dot trick.**

1/3 = 0.3̇. 5/11 = 0.4̇5̇. 1/7 = 0.1̇42857̇. The dots show where the repeating pattern starts and ends.

**Check: Is 1/4 a repeating decimal?**

No! 1/4 is 0.25. Since 4 is just 2 × 2, the decimal stops.

### 7.2 Ordering fractions

**Key idea: How do we compare fractions?**

Make the bottom numbers the same! It's like cutting two different pizzas into the same number of slices so you can compare them fairly.

**Recall: Which is smaller, −8/3 or −2 4/7?**

−8/3 is −2 14/21 and −2 4/7 is −2 12/21. Since −14 is further left on the number line, −8/3 is smaller.

**Check: Which is larger, 3/5 or 2/3?**

2/3! If we cut both into 15 slices, 3/5 is 9/15, and 2/3 is 10/15.

### 7.3 Subtracting mixed numbers

**Key idea: How do we subtract mixed numbers?**

Turn them into top-heavy (improper) fractions first! Make the bottom numbers the same, subtract the top numbers, and turn it back into a mixed number at the end.

**Recall: Work out 6 1/3 − 2 4/9.**

19/3 − 22/9 = 57/9 − 22/9 = 35/9 = 3 8/9.

**Check: Work out 3 1/4 − 1 1/2.**

13/4 − 6/4 = 7/4 = 1 3/4.

### 7.4 Multiplying an integer by a mixed number

**Key idea: How can we multiply a whole number by a mixed number easily?**

Split it up! Multiply the whole number part, then multiply the fraction part, and add them together.

**Recall: Work out 4 × 20 2/3.**

4 × 20 is 80. 4 × 2/3 is 8/3. Add them: 80 + 8/3 = 82 2/3.

**Check: Work out 6 × 2 1/3.**

6 × 7/3 = 14.

### 7.5 Dividing an integer by a fraction

**Key idea: How do we divide by a fraction?**

Use the flip-and-multiply trick! Flip the second fraction upside down (its reciprocal) and change ÷ to ×.

**Recall: Work out 10 ÷ 3/4.**

10 × 4/3 = 40/3 = 13 1/3.

**Check: Why is dividing by 1/2 the same as multiplying by 2?**

Because there are two halves in every whole! (Like cutting a pizza in half gives you 2 pieces).

### 7.6 Making fraction calculations easier

**Key idea: What is the cross-multiply trick for subtracting?**

Multiply diagonally! a/b − c/d = (ad − bc) / bd.

**Recall: Work out 4/5 − 3/4.**

(4 × 4 − 3 × 5) / (5 × 4) = (16 − 15) / 20 = 1/20.

**Check: Work out 5/6 × 9/10 by cancelling first.**

1/2 × 3/2 = 3/4. Cancelling divides the top and bottom by the same number, so the value stays the same.

## 8 Shapes and symmetry

*Geometry and measure*

Let's explore the world of shapes! We'll learn how to classify 4-sided shapes, measure around circles, and look at 3D objects from different angles.

### 8.1 Quadrilaterals and polygons

**Key idea: What makes a polygon 'regular'?**

It's perfect! All its sides are the exact same length, and all its inside angles are the same size. If it has 5 sides, it has 5 lines of symmetry!

**Recall: What's the difference between a rectangle and a rhombus?**

A rectangle has four 90° right angles (like a door). A rhombus is like a squashed square—all four sides are equal, but the angles don't have to be 90°.

**Check: A regular hexagon has how many lines of symmetry?**

6 lines of symmetry, and you can spin it 6 times before it gets back to the start (rotational symmetry of order 6).

### 8.2 The circumference of a circle

**Key idea: What is the 'circumference'?**

It's the distance all the way around the outside of a circle! The formula is C = π × d (pi times the diameter). Pi (π) is a magic number close to 3.14.

**Recall: Find the circumference of a circle with diameter 10 cm. Use π = 3.14.**

10 × 3.14 = 31.4 cm.

**Check: How do you find the perimeter of a half-circle (semicircle)?**

Find half the curved circumference, and don't forget to add the straight flat bottom (the diameter)!

### 8.3 3D shapes

**Key idea: What is Euler’s magic formula for 3D shapes?**

Faces + Vertices = Edges + 2! (Faces are flat sides, vertices are pointy corners, and edges are the straight lines where faces meet).

**Recall: What are the three ways to look at a 3D shape?**

The 'plan' is looking from like a bird from above. The 'front elevation' is looking straight at the front. The 'side elevation' is looking from the side.

**Check: A box is 7 cm long, 3 cm deep, and 2 cm tall. What does the bird's-eye 'plan' view look like?**

A flat rectangle that is 7 cm by 3 cm.

## 9 Sequences and functions

*Algebra*

Let's crack the code of number patterns! We'll learn how to predict the next number in a sequence and how to use a 'function machine' to turn inputs into outputs.

### 9.1 Generating sequences

**Key idea: What is a 'term-to-term' rule?**

It's the secret instruction that tells you how to jump from one number to the next one in the line.

**Recall: The first number is 4. The rule is 'multiply by 3 and add 2'. What are the next two numbers?**

3 × 4 + 2 = 14, then 3 × 14 + 2 = 44.

**Check: The first number is 3 and the rule is 'add 5'. What are the next three numbers?**

8, 13, 18.

### 9.2 Finding rules for sequences

**Key idea: How do we find the 'nth term' rule when the pattern grows by the same amount?**

Look at the jumps! If it jumps by 2 every time, the rule starts with '2n'. Then figure out what you need to add or subtract to make the first number work.

**Recall: What is the jump rule for 7, 11, 15, 19?**

Add 4.

**Check: A sequence goes 20, 17, 14, 11. What is the jump rule?**

Subtract 3.

### 9.3 Using the nth term

**Key idea: How do we write the 'nth term' rule?**

If the jumps are +3, write 3n. Then ask: what would the 'zero' term be before the sequence started? Add that on!

**Recall: Find the nth term of 7, 10, 13, 16, and the 20th term.**

The jump is +3, so it's 3n. The number before 7 would be 4. So the rule is 3n + 4. The 20th term is 3 × 20 + 4 = 64.

**Check: The nth term rule is 4n − 1. What is the 10th term?**

4 × 10 - 1 = 39.

### 9.4 Representing simple functions

**Key idea: What is a function machine?**

It's like a robot factory! You put a number in (x), the robot does some math to it, and spits a new number out (y).

**Recall: What is the reverse (inverse) of y = 2x + 7?**

Run the machine backwards! Undo the +7 with a -7, then undo the ×2 with a ÷2. So: x = (y − 7) / 2.

**Check: An input of 5 goes through a machine that says '-2' then '×4'. What is the output?**

(5 - 2) = 3. Then 3 × 4 = 12.

## 10 Percentages

*Number*

Let's go shopping! We'll learn how to calculate discounts in a sale and price increases using a super-fast 'multiplier' trick.

### 10.1 Percentage increases and decreases

**Key idea: How do you find a percentage change?**

Find the difference first! Then divide it by the ORIGINAL starting price, and multiply by 100 to make it a percentage.

**Recall: A ticket rises from £75 to £105. What is the percentage increase?**

The difference is £30. Divide by the start price: 30 ÷ 75 = 0.4. Multiply by 100 = 40%.

**Check: Increase £80 by 15%.**

10% is £8, 5% is £4. So 15% is £12. Add it on: £80 + £12 = £92.

### 10.2 Using a multiplier

**Key idea: What is the 'multiplier' trick?**

Instead of finding the percentage and adding it on, do it in one step! For a 35% increase, multiply by 1.35. For a 15% decrease (sale!), multiply by 0.85 (because 100% - 15% = 85%).

**Recall: Increase 240 by 35% using the multiplier trick.**

240 × 1.35 = 324.

**Check: Decrease 750 by 15% using the multiplier trick.**

750 × 0.85 = 637.50.

## 11 Graphs

*Algebra*

Let's draw some math! We'll learn how to turn rules into pictures, measure how steep a line is, and read stories from graphs.

### 11.1 Functions

**Key idea: How can a math formula describe a real-world cost?**

Imagine renting a bike. There's a starting fee (c), plus a cost for every hour (m). The formula is Cost = (m × hours) + c!

**Recall: A digger costs £35 to hire plus £10 a day. Write the cost for n days.**

Cost = 10n + 35.

**Check: For the rule y = 2x, what is y when x = 5?**

10. (Because 2 × 5 = 10).

### 11.2 Plotting graphs

**Key idea: How do you draw the line for y = 2x − 1?**

Make a quick table of x and y numbers, plot the dots on a grid, and join them up with a ruler!

**Recall: Complete the table: if x = 0, 1, 2 for the rule y = 3x + 1.**

y = 1, 4, 7. (Just multiply x by 3 and add 1).

**Check: Why should you always plot at least three dots for a straight line?**

Two dots will always make a straight line, even if you made a mistake! A third dot proves your math is right.

### 11.3 Gradient and intercept

**Key idea: In the famous rule y = mx + c, what do m and c mean?**

'm' is the gradient (how steep the hill is). 'c' is the y-intercept (where the line crashes into the y-axis).

**Recall: Where does the line y = 2x + 1 cross the y-axis?**

At 1! (The coordinate is 0, 1).

**Check: A line crosses the y-axis at 4 and goes up 2 steps for every 1 step across. What is its equation?**

y = 2x + 4.

### 11.4 Interpreting graphs

**Key idea: How do you read a real-life graph?**

If the line is flat, nothing is changing! If it's steep, things are changing super fast. If two lines cross, they are exactly equal at that moment.

**Recall: A distance–time graph is totally flat horizontally. What is happening?**

The object is standing completely still! (Speed is zero).

**Check: A graph changes £5 into $40. How many dollars do you get for £1?**

$8. (Because 40 ÷ 5 = 8).

## 12 Ratio and proportion

*Number*

Let's share things fairly! We'll learn how to split money or sweets using ratios, and how to scale up recipes using proportion.

### 12.1 Simplifying ratios

**Key idea: How do you simplify a ratio?**

Just like a fraction! Find the biggest number that divides into both sides. But remember: both sides MUST be in the same units first (like both in cm).

**Recall: Simplify 2 m : 50 cm.**

First, change 2m to 200cm. So it's 200 : 50. Divide both by 50 to get 4 : 1.

**Check: Simplify 1.5 : 2.**

Multiply both by 10 to get rid of the decimal: 15 : 20. Then divide by 5 to get 3 : 4.

### 12.2 Sharing in a ratio

**Key idea: How do you share money in a ratio?**

Add up all the parts to find the total number of 'shares'. Divide the money by the total shares to find what ONE share is worth. Then multiply!

**Recall: Share £840 in the ratio 2 : 3 : 1.**

Total parts = 6. One part = 840 ÷ 6 = £140. The shares are 2×140 (£280), 3×140 (£420), and 1×140 (£140).

**Check: Share £60 in the ratio 2 : 3.**

Total parts = 5. One part = £12. The shares are £24 and £36.

### 12.3 Ratio and direct proportion

**Key idea: What does 'direct proportion' mean?**

It means if you buy twice as many, it costs twice as much! They grow at the exact same speed.

**Recall: How do you compare which paint mix is redder?**

Turn them into fractions of the WHOLE mix. Mix A is 3 parts red and 4 parts white (total 7), so it's 3/7 red. Mix B is 2 parts red and 3 parts white (total 5), so it's 2/5 red. Then compare the fractions!

**Check: 5 pens cost £2. What do 15 pens cost?**

15 is three times as many pens, so it costs three times as much: £6.

## 13 Probability

*Statistics and probability*

Let's predict the future! We'll learn how to calculate the chance of winning a game, and why flipping a coin doesn't ALWAYS mean you get exactly half heads.

### 13.1 Calculating probabilities

**Key idea: How do you find the chance of something happening?**

Count the winning outcomes and divide by ALL the possible outcomes. If the chance of winning is 0.3, the chance of losing is 1 - 0.3 = 0.7.

**Recall: Two fair dice are rolled. What is the probability that the scores add up to 6?**

There are 36 possible outcomes. The winning pairs are (1,5), (2,4), (3,3), (4,2) and (5,1). That's 5 winners, so the chance is 5/36.

**Check: The probability of rain is 0.3. What is the probability it does NOT rain?**

0.7. (Because 1 - 0.3 = 0.7).

### 13.2 Experimental and theoretical probabilities

**Key idea: What is 'experimental' probability?**

It's what ACTUALLY happens when you play the game! You divide the number of wins by the number of times you played. The more you play, the closer it gets to the math theory.

**Recall: A coin lands heads 62 times in 100 flips. What is the experimental probability (relative frequency)?**

62 ÷ 100 = 0.62.

**Check: Why is it better to flip a coin 1000 times instead of just 10 times?**

Because weird runs of luck (like getting 5 heads in a row) get washed out when you do it lots of times!

## 14 Position and transformation

*Geometry and measure*

Let's move things around! We'll learn how to navigate like a pirate using bearings, and how to slide, flip, spin, and shrink shapes on a grid.

### 14.1 Bearings

**Key idea: What are the three rules for a pirate's bearing?**

1. Always start facing North (000°). 2. Always turn clockwise. 3. Always write three numbers (like 045° or 090°).

**Recall: The bearing from A to B is 070°. What is the reverse bearing from B back to A?**

070° is small, so add 180° to turn all the way around! The reverse is 250°.

**Check: The bearing from A to B is 235°. What is the reverse bearing?**

235° is big, so subtract 180°. The reverse is 055°.

### 14.2 The midpoint of a line segment

**Key idea: How do you find the exact middle of a line?**

Find the average! Add the two x-coordinates and chop in half. Then add the two y-coordinates and chop in half.

**Recall: What is the midpoint of (10, 4) and (−4, −6)?**

x is (10 + -4)/2 = 3. y is (4 + -6)/2 = -1. So the middle is (3, −1).

**Check: What is the midpoint of (2, 6) and (8, 10)?**

(5, 8).

### 14.3 Translating 2D shapes

**Key idea: How do we write a 'slide' (translation)?**

We use a tall bracket called a vector! The top number is Left/Right (positive is Right). The bottom number is Up/Down (positive is Up).

**Recall: A vector has a 3 on top and a -2 on the bottom. What does it do?**

It slides the shape 3 steps Right and 2 steps Down.

**Check: The point (1, 4) is translated by 2 across and 5 up. Where does it land?**

(3, 9).

### 14.4 Reflecting shapes

**Key idea: How do mirrors work on a grid?**

The shape flips over! Every point on the new shape is exactly the same distance from the mirror line as the old shape.

**Recall: What do the mirror lines x = 2 and y = 3 look like?**

x = 2 is a straight up-and-down vertical wall. y = 3 is a flat horizontal floor.

**Check: Reflect (2, 5) in the diagonal mirror line y = x.**

Just swap them! It becomes (5, 2).

### 14.5 Rotating shapes

**Key idea: What three things do you need to spin a shape?**

1. The angle (like 90°). 2. The direction (clockwise or anticlockwise). 3. The centre point to pin it down on!

**Recall: Rotate (3, 1) by 90° anticlockwise around the center (0, 0).**

(−1, 3).

**Check: A rotation of 180° around the center sends (4, −2) where?**

(−4, 2).

### 14.6 Enlarging shapes

**Key idea: How do you shrink or grow a shape?**

You need a 'scale factor' (how many times bigger) and a 'centre' to grow it from. If the scale factor is 1/2, the shape shrinks to half the size!

**Recall: What happens if the centre of enlargement is a corner of the shape?**

That corner stays still. It is an invariant point.

**Check: A side of 4 cm is enlarged with scale factor 2.5. How long is the image side?**

10 cm. A scale factor between 0 and 1 makes a smaller image.

## 15 Distance, area and volume

*Geometry and measure*

Let's measure the world! We'll learn how to switch between miles and kilometres, find the area of weird shapes like trapeziums, and calculate the space inside 3D prisms.

### 15.1 Converting between miles and kilometres

**Key idea: How do you switch between kilometres and miles?**

Remember the magic fraction: 5 miles is about exactly 8 kilometres! To change miles to km, divide by 5 and multiply by 8.

**Recall: Convert 20 miles to kilometres.**

(20 ÷ 5) × 8 = 32 km.

**Check: Convert 72 km to miles.**

Go backwards! (72 ÷ 8) × 5 = 45 miles.

### 15.2 The area of a parallelogram and a trapezium

**Key idea: What are the area formulas?**

Parallelogram (slanted rectangle): base × straight height. Trapezium (flat top and bottom): Add the top and bottom, chop in half, then multiply by height! ½(a + b) × h.

**Recall: A parallelogram has base 9 cm and straight height 4 cm. Area?**

9 × 4 = 36 cm².

**Check: A trapezium has parallel sides 6 cm and 10 cm, and height 5 cm. Area?**

½(6 + 10) = 8. Then 8 × 5 = 40 cm².

### 15.3 Calculating the volume of triangular prisms

**Key idea: How do you find the space inside (volume) of a prism?**

Find the area of the front face (the cross-section), then multiply it by how long the shape is!

**Recall: The front triangle has an area of 12 cm², and the prism is 10 cm long. What is the volume?**

12 × 10 = 120 cm³.

**Check: A triangular prism has a triangle base 6 cm and height 4 cm, and a length of 8 cm. Volume?**

Triangle area = ½ × 6 × 4 = 12. Volume = 12 × 8 = 96 cm³.

### 15.4 Calculating the surface area of triangular prisms and pyramids

**Key idea: What is 'surface area'?**

Imagine wrapping the shape in paper! It's the total area of all the flat faces added together.

**Recall: How do you find the surface area of a triangular prism (like a Toblerone box)?**

Find the area of the two triangle ends, and add the area of the three rectangle sides!

**Check: A square-based pyramid has a base area of 25, and 4 triangles that are 10 each. Total surface area?**

25 + 10 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 65.

## 16 Interpreting and discussing results

*Statistics*

Let's read the stories behind the numbers! We'll look at pie charts, line graphs, and figure out how to find the 'average' without getting tricked by weird numbers.

### 16.1 Interpreting and drawing frequency diagrams

**Key idea: Why do some bar charts have gaps and some don't?**

If the data is counted (like shoe sizes), the bars have gaps. If the data is measured continuously (like height), the bars touch because there are no gaps between heights!

**Recall: A bar is twice as tall as another. What does that mean?**

It means exactly twice as many people chose that thing (as long as the graph starts at zero!).

**Check: Why are there gaps between bars for shoe sizes but not for heights?**

Shoe size is discrete (you can't have a size 4.123). Height is continuous, so the groups touch.

### 16.2 Time series graphs

**Key idea: What is a 'time series' graph?**

It's a graph where time goes along the bottom. You join the dots in order to see if things are going up or down over time (the 'trend').

**Recall: What is a 'trend'?**

The overall direction the line is going (like a slow rise), ignoring the little bouncy zig-zags along the way.

**Check: Why join the points in a time series but not in a scatter graph?**

Time happens in order! A scatter graph just compares two random things that don't follow a path.

### 16.3 Stem-and-leaf diagrams

**Key idea: How does a stem-and-leaf diagram work?**

It's a clever way to list numbers! The 'stem' is the first digit (like the tens), and the 'leaves' are the last digits (like the ones). 1 | 9 means 19.

**Recall: Using that key, what temperatures are in the row 2 | 1 1 5 6 7 9?**

21, 21, 25, 26, 27, and 29.

**Check: Why is a stem-and-leaf diagram useful?**

You can see the shape of the data (like a bar chart on its side) but you can still read every single exact number!

### 16.4 Pie charts

**Key idea: How do you draw a slice of a pie chart?**

Find the fraction of people who chose it, then multiply by 360° (a full circle).

**Recall: 20 people were asked, and 5 chose red. What angle is the red slice?**

5 out of 20 is a quarter (1/4). A quarter of 360° is 90° (a right angle!).

**Check: A slice is 120° and 30 people were surveyed in total. How many people does the slice represent?**

120° is one-third of 360°. One-third of 30 people is 10 people.

### 16.5 Representing data

**Key idea: Which graph should I use?**

Change over time = time series. A share of a whole = pie chart. Exact numbers = stem-and-leaf. Two linked things = scatter graph.

**Recall: What must every good graph have?**

A title, labels on the sides, and units (like 'cm' or 'minutes').

**Check: Why can a 3D pie chart trick you?**

The slices at the front look way bigger than they actually are!

### 16.6 Using statistics

**Key idea: What are the Mean, Median, Mode and Range?**

Mean: add them all and divide. Median: line them up and pick the middle one. Mode: the most popular one. Range: biggest minus smallest.

**Recall: Which average is best if there is one crazy outlier (like a giant number)?**

The median! The crazy number will pull the mean way up, but the median just stays safe in the middle.

**Check: Two classes took a test. Class A had a range of 10. Class B had a range of 30. What does that mean?**

Class A's scores were much closer together (more consistent). Class B's scores were spread out everywhere!
