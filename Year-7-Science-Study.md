# Year 7 Science

**Learner’s Book 8**

Stage 8 science: respiration, materials, forces, ecosystems, atoms and climate, light, diet, reactions and magnetism, one unit at a time.

## 1 Respiration

*Biology*

How air gets into the lungs, how gases are exchanged, how breathing is controlled, and how cells release energy.

### 1.1 The human respiratory system

**Key idea: Which organs make up the respiratory system?**

Nose and mouth, larynx (voice box, with the vocal cords), trachea (windpipe), bronchi, bronchioles and air sacs (alveoli), plus the diaphragm and rib muscles that move air.

**Recall: What do the rings of cartilage do?**

They keep the trachea and bronchi open so air can always pass.

**Check: Why is the lining of the airways moist and covered in mucus?**

Dust and microbes stick to the mucus. Cilia sweep it up and out of the lungs.

### 1.2 Gas exchange

**Key idea: Where does gas exchange happen?**

In the alveoli. Oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out.

**Recall: Name three features that make alveoli efficient.**

A huge surface area, walls one cell thick, and a rich blood supply.

**Check: Why is oxygen still able to enter the blood when you are resting?**

Blood arriving at the lungs has a lower oxygen concentration than the air in the alveoli, so oxygen diffuses in.

### 1.3 Breathing

**Key idea: How does air get into the lungs?**

The diaphragm contracts and flattens, the ribcage lifts, lung volume rises and air is drawn in.

**Recall: What happens when you breathe out?**

The diaphragm and rib muscles relax, the chest gets smaller, and air is pushed out.

**Check: Why does exercise make you breathe faster and deeper?**

Muscles need more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide, so gas exchange has to speed up.

### 1.4 Respiration

**Key idea: What is respiration?**

A chemical reaction in cells that releases energy from glucose. It is not the same thing as breathing.

**Recall: Write the word equation for aerobic respiration.**

glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy).

**Check: Where does respiration happen?**

Inside cells. Breathing only moves air; respiration is the reaction that releases energy.

## 2 Properties of materials

*Chemistry*

Dissolving, how much solute a solvent can hold, how to plan a fair solubility test, and how chromatography separates mixtures.

### 2.1 Dissolving

**Key idea: What happens when a solid dissolves?**

Its particles separate and spread evenly through the solvent. The solute seems to disappear, but it is still there.

**Recall: Solute, solvent, solution — which is which?**

The solute dissolves. The solvent does the dissolving. Together they make a solution.

**Check: Why does stirring or warming often speed up dissolving?**

Particles collide more often, so the solid breaks up into the solvent faster.

### 2.2 Solutions and solubility

**Key idea: What is solubility?**

The mass of solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a stated temperature, until no more will dissolve.

**Recall: What is a saturated solution?**

One that holds as much dissolved solute as it can at that temperature. Extra solute stays undissolved.

**Check: How does temperature usually affect the solubility of a solid?**

Most solids are more soluble in warmer water. Gases are usually less soluble when the liquid is warmer.

### 2.3 Planning a solubility investigation

**Key idea: What must stay the same in a fair solubility test?**

The volume of solvent, how you decide the solution is saturated, and the method of stirring. Only temperature (or the solute) should change.

**Recall: What would you measure?**

The mass of solute that dissolves, and the temperature of the solvent.

**Check: Why repeat the test?**

Repeats show whether a result is reliable. A single odd reading is easier to spot.

### 2.4 Paper chromatography

**Key idea: What is paper chromatography used for?**

Separating a mixture of soluble substances, such as the dyes in an ink.

**Recall: Why must the start line be drawn in pencil?**

Pencil does not dissolve in the solvent. Ink from a pen would run and spoil the result.

**Check: A spot does not move. What does that tell you?**

That substance does not dissolve in the solvent you chose.

## 3 Forces and energy

*Physics*

How forces change motion, how to calculate speed, moments, pressure, and how particle movement explains diffusion.

### 3.1 Forces and motion

**Key idea: What can a force do?**

A force can start, stop, speed up, slow down or change the direction of motion, or change an object’s shape.

**Recall: What is a resultant force?**

The single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting together.

**Check: A book is still on a table. What is the resultant force?**

Zero. The support force from the table balances the weight.

### 3.2 Speed

**Key idea: How do you calculate average speed?**

Average speed = distance travelled ÷ time taken. A common unit is m/s.

**Recall: A cyclist travels 120 m in 20 s. What is the average speed?**

6 m/s.

**Check: Why is a speedometer not the same as average speed?**

It shows the speed at one moment. Average speed uses the whole journey.

### 3.3 Describing movement

**Key idea: What does the steepness of a distance–time graph show?**

Speed. A steeper straight line means a higher constant speed. A flat line means the object is still.

**Recall: What does a curved distance–time graph mean?**

The speed is changing.

**Check: How do you find the speed from a straight section?**

Speed = change in distance ÷ change in time for that section.

### 3.4 Turning forces

**Key idea: What is a moment?**

The turning effect of a force. Moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot.

**Recall: When is an object balanced?**

When the clockwise moment equals the anticlockwise moment.

**Check: A 4 N weight sits 0.3 m from a pivot. What moment does it produce?**

1.2 N m.

### 3.5 Pressure between solids

**Key idea: How do you calculate pressure?**

Pressure = force ÷ area. A bigger area gives a smaller pressure for the same force.

**Recall: Why are snowshoes wide?**

They spread the weight over a larger area, so the pressure on the snow is smaller.

**Check: A 50 N force acts on 0.02 m². What is the pressure?**

2500 N/m² (also called 2500 Pa).

### 3.6 Pressure in liquids and gases

**Key idea: How does pressure in a liquid change with depth?**

It increases with depth, because more liquid is pressing down.

**Recall: Why does a gas exert pressure?**

Its particles collide with the walls of the container.

**Check: What happens to gas pressure if you squeeze the same gas into a smaller volume, at the same temperature?**

Pressure rises, because particles hit the walls more often.

### 3.7 Particles on the move

**Key idea: What is diffusion?**

The spreading of particles from a region where they are crowded to one where they are less crowded.

**Recall: Why does diffusion happen faster in a gas than in a liquid?**

Gas particles move faster and are further apart, so they spread more quickly.

**Check: Why does the smell of perfume fill a room even with no wind?**

Perfume particles diffuse through the air by themselves.

## 4 Ecosystems

*Biology*

The Sonoran Desert food web, other ecosystems (mangrove, Arctic ice, rice paddy), invasive species in New Zealand, and how DDT builds up along a food chain.

### 4.1 The Sonoran desert

**Key idea: What is an ecosystem?**

A network of interactions between living organisms and the non-living things around them (light, water, soil, air, temperature).

**Recall: Why are many desert animals nocturnal?**

Night is cooler, so they lose less water and do not overheat.

**Check: What do the arrows in a food web represent?**

The flow of energy / who eats whom. The arrow points to the eater.

### 4.2 Different ecosystems

**Key idea: Is an ecosystem only a place?**

No. It is the living and non-living parts and how they interact.

**Recall: What are the producers under Arctic sea ice?**

Tiny algae (single-celled plants) growing on the underside of the ice.

**Check: Name one habitat inside a rice-paddy ecosystem.**

Any of: shallow flood water, muddy banks, rice plants, irrigation canals, air above the field.

### 4.3 Intruders in an ecosystem

**Key idea: What is a native species?**

A species that belongs in an ecosystem naturally — it was not brought by people.

**Recall: Why are stoats a problem in New Zealand but not in Europe?**

In Europe they evolved with prey that can cope. In New Zealand flightless birds and ground nests had no mammal predators.

**Check: What kind of species is buffelgrass in Arizona?**

An introduced / invasive species — it is growing where it does not belong.

### 4.4 Bioaccumulation

**Key idea: What is DDT?**

An insecticide (a chemical that kills insects). It was used against mosquitoes and crop pests.

**Recall: Why do eggs fail to hatch after DDT?**

DDT is stored in the female bird. It makes the shells thin, so eggs break.

**Check: Why is a cormorant more poisoned than a minnow?**

It eats many contaminated minnows, so all their DDT adds together in one bird.

## 5 Materials and their structure

*Chemistry · Earth*

Atoms and how scientists modelled them, what ‘pure’ means (gold carat, seawater, diamonds), weather versus climate, ice ages, and how the atmosphere traps heat.

### 5.1 The structure of the atom

**Key idea: What is inside an atom?**

Nucleus of protons (+) and neutrons (0); electrons (−) around it. Almost all mass is in the nucleus. Electrostatic attraction holds the atom together. Mostly empty space.

**Recall: Gold foil**

Most particles passed through (empty space); a few bounced (tiny dense nucleus). Thomson: electron. Rutherford: nucleus and proton. Chadwick: neutron (1932).

**Check: Mass number**

8 protons + 8 neutrons = 16. Proton number decides the element.

### 5.2 Purity

**Key idea: Pure, and gold carat**

Pure = one substance. 24 carat = pure gold. 18 carat = 18/24 = 75% gold.

**Recall: Seawater**

About 96.5% water, 3.5% mixed salts; only ~68% of the salt is sodium chloride.

**Check: Air**

Clean air is a mixture. Pure diamond is only carbon; other atoms change colour.

### 5.3 Weather and climate

**Key idea**

Weather = now (minutes–days). Climate = average over 30+ years. Meteorology vs climatology.

**Recall**

Temperature, rain, wind, cloud, humidity, visibility. Same time each day; temperature in the shade.

**Check**

A cold week is weather, not proof about climate. Zones: polar, temperate, arid, tropical, Mediterranean.

### 5.4 Climate and ice ages

**Key idea**

Ice age = long cold chapter. Glacial = ice far from poles. Interglacial (now) = ice mainly at poles. 25 000 years ago ice covered much more land.

**Recall**

Boulders, rock scratches, pollen in peat, frozen mammoths, cold-climate fossils.

**Check**

Several ice ages. Today is an interglacial, not a glacial.

### 5.5 Atmosphere and climate

**Key idea**

Early air mostly CO₂ from volcanoes. Today ~78% nitrogen, ~21% oxygen after photosynthesis.

**Recall**

Burning fossil fuels, deforestation and cement release CO₂. carbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide.

**Check**

Greenhouse gases (CO₂, methane) act like a blanket and trap heat.

## 6 Light

*Physics · Earth and space*

How light reflects from a plane mirror, refracts in glass and water, splits into a rainbow, mixes as colours on a screen, and what galaxies and leftover space rocks tell us about the Universe.

### 6.1 Reflection

**Key idea: State the law of reflection.**

The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (i = r). Measure both from the normal, not from the mirror surface. A plane mirror is flat.

**Recall: What kit does the book use to test reflection?**

A ray box, a plane mirror, a protractor and a set square to draw the normal at 90° (perpendicular) to the mirror.

**Check: How do motorcycle wing mirrors help a rider, and why is crumpled foil a poor mirror?**

Light from a car behind reflects from the plane mirror into the rider’s eyes. Foil is shiny but crumpled, so rays scatter and you do not get a sharp image.

### 6.2 Refraction

**Key idea: What is refraction?**

Light changes speed between materials and can change direction. Air about 300 000 km/s, water 225 000 km/s, glass 200 000 km/s.

**Recall: Which way does a ray bend, and where do we use refraction?**

Air into glass or water: towards the normal. Glass or water into air: away from the normal. Glasses, phone cameras and wet windows all use or show this.

**Check: Why does a pencil look broken, and how can a hidden coin appear?**

Rays leaving water bend away from the normal so the underwater part looks shifted. Pouring water into a bowl refracts rays from the coin so they reach your eye.

### 6.3 Making rainbows

**Key idea: How did Newton show white light is mixed?**

In 1666 he used a triangular glass prism. White light dispersed into a spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.

**Recall: Which colour bends most, and which least?**

Violet is refracted through the largest angle. Red is refracted through the smallest.

**Check: What three things do you need to see a rainbow?**

The Sun shining, drops of water in the air, and the Sun behind you so the drops can reflect light towards you. A hose spray on a sunny day works the same way.

### 6.4 Colours of light

**Key idea: What are the primary colours of light?**

Red, green and blue. Adding: R+G=yellow, R+B=magenta, B+G=cyan, RGB=white. Phone and TV pixels work this way.

**Recall: How does a coloured filter work?**

It transmits its colour and absorbs the rest (subtraction). Traffic lights use white lamps plus red, yellow or green filters.

**Check: Why can a red cube look black in green light?**

It reflects only red. Green is absorbed, so almost no light comes back. A yellow flower looks yellow because it reflects yellow.

### 6.5 Galaxies

**Key idea: What is a galaxy?**

Dust, gas, stars and solar systems held by gravity. The milky band at night is our spiral galaxy, the Milky Way, seen from inside.

**Recall: Name the three galaxy shapes.**

Spiral, elliptical and irregular. The closest known neighbour is the Canis Major Dwarf Galaxy (elliptical).

**Check: How do scientists estimate how many galaxies there are?**

They count galaxies in one patch of sky and scale up, like counting sand grains in a cup then estimating a whole bucket. One estimate is about 100 billion galaxies.

### 6.6 Rocks in space

**Key idea: What is an asteroid?**

A rock that orbits the Sun. Most sit in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. They are leftover from the formation of the Solar System.

**Recall: What is special about Ceres and Itokawa?**

Ceres (1801) is the largest, about 975 km, round and cratered. Itokawa is a small potato-shaped rubble pile that a spacecraft sampled in 2005.

**Check: Why do asteroids hit Earth, and how often is a large impact?**

Earth’s gravity pulls them, and many orbits pass nearby. A large impact is thought to happen about every 130 000 years. Smaller ones often break up before they reach the ground.

## 7 Diet and growth

*Biology*

The six nutrients, a balanced diet and fibre, growth versus development, smoking, and how joints and antagonistic muscles move the arm.

### 7.1 Nutrients

Six types: carbohydrate, fat/oil, protein, vitamins, minerals, water. Fibre is useful but not one of the six.

- **Nutrient:** substance in food you need to stay healthy.
- **Starch → glucose → energy** (iodine: orange-brown → blue-black if starch is present).
- Protein: growth, repair, haemoglobin, antibodies.
- Fat: energy and cell membranes.
- Vitamin A: night vision. Vitamin C: skin/gums (scurvy if missing). Vitamin D + calcium: bones (rickets if too little D). Iron: haemoglobin (anaemia if missing). Water: dissolve chemicals and carry blood.

### 7.2 A balanced diet

- **Diet:** food you eat each day.
- **Balanced diet:** all six nutrients + the right energy.
- **1 MJ = 1 000 000 J.**
- **Fibre:** cellulose; not digested; prevents constipation.
- Triangle: plenty of starchy food, lots of fruit/veg, some protein foods, few sweets/fried foods.

### 7.3 Growth, development and health

- **Growth:** more cells (needs protein + energy).
- **Development:** new organs and skills. **Embryo:** early ball of cells.
- Exercise uses energy and strengthens heart and muscle.
- Smoking: **nicotine** (addictive), **tar** (cancer), **carbon monoxide** (blocks haemoglobin), **particulates** (damage alveoli).

### 7.4 Moving the body

- **Hinge joint** (elbow): one direction. **Ball-and-socket** (shoulder): circular.
- **Contraction:** muscle gets shorter. Muscles only pull.
- **Tendon:** muscle to bone. **Ligament:** bone to bone.
- **Antagonistic pair:** biceps bends, triceps straightens.

## 8 Chemical reactions

*Chemistry*

Reactions that give out or take in heat, and how metals react with oxygen, water and acids.

### 8.1 Exothermic reactions

**Key idea: What is an exothermic reaction?**

A chemical change that transfers energy to the surroundings, usually as heat (sometimes light or sound). The mixture and the air around it get warmer, so a thermometer reading rises. Memory hook: *exo = exit* — energy leaves the reaction.

**Recall: Give everyday examples.**

Combustion: campfire, candle, or hydrogen in a model bottle rocket (hydrogen + oxygen → water; atoms rearrange and energy is given out as heat, light and sound). Also acid + alkali neutralisation; air-activated hand warmers; some plasters setting; respiration in cells (a slow, controlled energy release, not a flame).

**Check: How would you show a reaction is exothermic?**

Measure temperature before and after with the same thermometer. Calculate ΔT = T_final − T_initial. A positive rise (for example 21 °C → 48 °C) means energy has been given out. Repeat and take a mean. Do not rely on “it felt warm”.

**Example: carbon burning**

carbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide, and energy is given out as heat and light. Oxygen is a *reactant* on the left, not a product. New substances form, so this is a chemical change.

**Particles in one sentence**

More energy is released when new bonds form in the products than was used to break bonds in the reactants. The spare energy heats the surroundings.

### 8.2 Endothermic reactions

**Key idea: What is an endothermic reaction?**

A chemical change that takes in thermal energy from the surroundings and stores it in chemical bonds. The temperature at the end is lower than at the start (negative ΔT).

**Recall: Why does sherbet make your mouth feel cooler?**

Sherbet is citric acid plus sodium hydrogen carbonate. Saliva starts the same reaction as in the test tube. Energy is taken from your mouth and carbon dioxide makes the fizz.

**Check: Is every temperature drop a chemical reaction?**

No. Melting ice or dissolving potassium chloride / ammonium nitrate takes in energy but makes no new substance. That is an endothermic process.

### 8.3 Metals and their reactions with oxygen

**Key idea: What forms when a metal burns or corrodes in air?**

A metal oxide. The metal has been oxidised.

**Recall: Which metals react most readily with oxygen?**

The reactive ones, such as magnesium and sodium. Gold hardly reacts at all.

**Check: Write a word equation for magnesium burning.**

magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide.

### 8.4 Reactions of metals with water

**Key idea: What do the most reactive metals do in water?**

They react to make a metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas.

**Recall: Which is more reactive with water, potassium or copper?**

Potassium. It reacts vigorously. Copper does not react with water.

**Check: How can you test the gas given off?**

A lit splint gives a squeaky pop if the gas is hydrogen.

### 8.5 Reactions of metals with dilute acids

**Key idea: What is made when a metal reacts with a dilute acid?**

A salt and hydrogen. The salt depends on the acid: hydrochloric acid makes chlorides.

**Recall: Write the word equation for zinc and hydrochloric acid.**

zinc + hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride + hydrogen.

**Check: Why is copper used for water pipes when magnesium would not be?**

Copper is low in the reactivity series, so it does not react with water or dilute acids in ordinary use.

## 9 Magnetism

*Physics*

Magnetic fields around magnets, how Earth acts like a giant magnet, electromagnets you can switch, and fair tests of their strength.

### 9.1 Magnetic fields

**Key idea: What is a magnetic field?**

The space around a magnet where its effects can be detected (a steel paperclip is attracted). Strongest at the poles; weaker further away. Magnetic materials: iron, steel, nickel, cobalt — not copper or aluminium.

**Recall: Field line rules**

Join opposite poles; arrows N → S; lines must not touch or cross; closer lines = stronger field. A compass needle lines up with the lines. Horseshoe magnets concentrate the field in the gap.

**Check: How do two magnets interact?**

Opposite poles attract (field lines between them run the same way). Like poles repel (lines run opposite ways and bulge out).

### 9.2 The Earth as a giant magnet

**Key idea: Why does a compass point north?**

Earth has a magnetic field from its iron-rich core, similar to a bar magnet. A compass needle lines up with it. Geographic north is on the spin axis; magnetic north is nearby in the Arctic and moves slowly (~60 km/year).

**Recall: Why the north-seeking pole points north**

Outside a magnet, field lines go N → S. They enter Earth near geographic north, so that region acts like a magnetic south pole. Opposite poles attract. Aurorae: solar particles following the strong polar field. Birds also use the field.

**Check: Lab evidence**

Hang a bar magnet, or float a magnetised needle on cork, away from other magnets. Both settle north–south. Lodestone (magnetite) is a naturally occurring magnet.

### 9.3 Electromagnets

**Key idea: How do you make one?**

Coil of insulated wire around an iron core + current + switch. Switch off and it loses most of its magnetism. Reverse coil or cell to swap poles.

**Recall: Words**

Magnetic = attracted to a magnet. Magnetised = turned into a magnet. Permanent = field always on. Electromagnet = switchable.

**Check: Uses that need on/off**

Scrapyard crane, toaster, fire door, electric bell, motors, MRI.

### 9.4 Investigating electromagnets

**Key idea: Three factors**

Turns on the coil, size of current, core material (soft iron magnetises and demagnetises easily). Measure strength by paperclips held.

**Recall: Fair test**

Change one factor only. Short bursts of current. Repeat and average. Line graph for turns; bar chart for core materials.

**Check: Pattern**

More turns or more current → more clips, up to a limit. Iron/nickel core beats wood, rubber or aluminium.
